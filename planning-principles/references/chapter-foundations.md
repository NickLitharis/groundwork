# Planning foundations and decision examples

This reference records inspected sources behind the planning guidance. It distinguishes author advice from this skill’s synthesis; it does not claim that a particular plan format or this skill improves outcomes. Sources are references, not runtime dependencies.

## Inspected sources and boundaries

- **Karl Wiegers and Candase Hokanson, *Software Requirements Essentials* (2023).** Read actual passages from the publisher’s [official Pearson sample PDF](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780138190286/samplepages/9780138190286_Sample.pdf): Chapter 1 and Chapter 2’s opening and Practice 1, “Understand the problem before converging on a solution.” Also read [InformIT Chapter 1, “Good Practices for Requirements Engineering”](https://www.informit.com/articles/article.aspx?p=3172443&seqNum=2). The inspected text describes iterative/concurrent elicitation, analysis, specification, and validation, and tailors requirements work to uncertainty, risk, change rate, stakeholder access, and late-change cost. Chapter 2’s remaining practices and later chapters were not reviewed; do not attribute their contents here.
- **David Farley, *Modern Software Engineering* (2021).** Read the preface and Chapter 3, “Fundamentals of an Engineering Approach,” including “The Importance of Measurement,” in Pearson’s [official sample](https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780137314911/samplepages/9780137314911_Sample.pdf). The text frames engineering as empirical problem-solving and distinguishes useful outcome measurement from a direct productivity measure; described delivery associations are correlations, not causation. The book’s contents list iterative and experimental topics, but this source map makes no detailed claim about chapters whose prose was not inspected.
- **The SPACE of Developer Productivity (2021).** Read the ACM article [“The SPACE of Developer Productivity”](https://cacm.acm.org/practice/the-space-of-developer-productivity/). The authors caution against reducing productivity to activity or one metric and describe multiple dimensions. This is a measurement framework synthesizing prior research and practice, not proof that any one task plan or design method improves productivity.
- **DORA metrics guide, current page last updated 5 January 2026.** Read [DORA metrics](https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics/). It describes five delivery measures—change lead time, deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time, change fail rate, and deployment rework rate—and warns against optimizing one measure, turning metrics into targets, or comparing unlike applications. These are service/application-level delivery measures, not acceptance criteria or productivity scores for an individual coding task; the model evolves, so check the current guide before citing its version.
- **Eric S. Raymond, *The Art of Unix Programming* (2003).** The planning skill’s existing references cover inspected material in [chapters 4, 6, 8–9, 15–16, and 18](https://cdn.nakamotoinstitute.org/docs/taoup.pdf) for authoritative representations, transparency, generation, tooling, reuse, and audience-specific documentation, plus chapters 11–13 for interface, complexity, and optimization tradeoffs. These are Unix design arguments adapted to current repositories, not an imposed project-management process.

## Synthesis boundaries

Start from a need and an outcome, not a proposed implementation. Make requirements sufficiently clear for the next safe decision, then revisit them as evidence arrives. Plan detail and experiments in proportion to uncertainty, impact, reversibility, and coordination cost. Acceptance evidence should measure the promised behavior; activity counts such as commits, tickets, or lines of code are not substitutes for user or system outcomes. Do not import Google-scale practices or a DORA metric as an individual-task target. These are conditional recommendations synthesized from the inspected text, not universal requirements or guarantees.

## Decision examples: why and how to check

1. **Feature request or underlying need?** A request says “merge these two dashboards,” but the pain may be finding a particular answer rather than operating two systems. Clarify the user, task, and outcome before planning a rewrite; a small workflow observation or prototype may answer the consequential question sooner than a full specification. Check with the requester that the proposed success measure reflects the original pain, and identify what evidence would change the approach.
2. **Plan now or investigate an uncertain dependency?** A required external API may not support a needed operation. Time-box a minimal feasibility experiment with a specific question, a stopping point, and a decision rule before scheduling dependent implementation. Check the behavior against the actual API and constraints, not a mock alone; revise the plan if the assumption fails.
3. **How to show the rollout improved?** If a service change is meant to reduce user-visible delay, choose a representative latency measure and baseline under comparable conditions, then pair it with correctness and failure evidence. Do not use PR count or deployment frequency as proof that users benefited. Check that the metric tracks the intended service outcome, compare like workloads, and state what remains unmeasured; use organizational delivery metrics only when that is the decision’s scale and the measures fit.
